The Fox Who Kept the Light

There was once a fox who lived at the end of a long grey point of land, where the rocks went out into the sea and a white lighthouse stood at the very tip. The keeper of the lighthouse was an old woman with a red scarf, and every evening she climbed the stairs, one hundred and twelve of them, and lit the great lamp so that the boats would know where the rocks were.

The fox watched her from the long grass. He did not understand the light. It did not catch anything, and it could not be eaten, and yet the old woman tended it more carefully than she tended her own supper. Some nights the wind was so strong that the fox lay flat in his den and listened to the sea throwing itself at the stones, and still the light turned and turned above him, steady as a heartbeat.

One winter the old woman fell ill. The fox knew it before anyone, because the smell of woodsmoke stopped and the door stayed shut. That evening the sun went down and the tower stayed dark. Far out on the water the fox could see the small lamps of the fishing boats, coming home, coming closer to the rocks than they knew.

The fox was afraid of the tower. He had never been inside a house of any kind. But he remembered the old woman climbing, and he remembered how she held the long match, and he thought: if nobody lights it, then nobody will. So he pushed the door with his nose, and it opened, and he went up the stairs in the dark, counting nothing, only climbing.

At the top he found the old woman's matches in a tin by the lamp. He could not strike a match with his paws. He tried and tried, and the matches broke and fell. Down below, he heard the first boat sound its horn, a long, lost sound, the sound of someone asking where they were.

Then the fox did a clever thing. He carried the tin of matches down all one hundred and twelve stairs, across the yard, and scratched at the old woman's door until she woke. She was weak and her hands shook, but she understood at once. She leaned on the fox, and the fox leaned on the wall, and together they climbed the tower, stopping to breathe on every landing.

The light came on just as the boats reached the rocks. The fishermen saw it and turned, all of them, one after another, and slid safely past into the harbour. They never knew how close they had come, and they never knew who had climbed the stairs.

After that the old woman left the door of the tower open, and a bowl of something good on the bottom step every night. The fox came every evening at dusk and sat beside her while she lit the lamp. When she grew too old to climb, her granddaughter came to keep the light, and the fox sat beside her too.

People in the village say that the fox still lives at the end of the point, though by now it must be his grandchildren's grandchildren. They say that if the light ever goes out, a small red shape will be seen running toward the nearest lit window, as fast as it can go, to wake someone up. The fox never learned to light the lamp himself. He only learned that a light is worth keeping, and that you do not have to keep it alone.
