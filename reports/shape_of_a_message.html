Every language says it differently. They all say the same amount.

We measured the same book, the Bible, in 56 languages, plus 62 novels in English, French and German. This page walks through what we found about what a message is, once you strip away the words that carry it.

Genesis 1:1 in twelve of the translations we used. It's one sentence. The amber bars show how many characters each language needs for it, from 7 in Chinese to 51 in Thai.

Idea 1

Length lies. Information doesn't.

Count characters and languages look wildly different: Chinese needs less than a third of the characters English uses for the whole Bible. Now measure something else. Compress each Bible as tightly as a computer can, and count the bits that are left. That is the information that couldn't be squeezed out.

Length varies about fourfold. Information varies by about 7%. Thousands of translators, working independently over four centuries, landed on nearly the same information budget for each verse. The leftover 7% isn't random either: scripts that leave out vowels (Hebrew, Arabic) come in low, because readers supply the vowels themselves.

Idea 2

Strangers up close, neighbours from a distance

Why do languages look so different and carry the same amount? Let the compressor remember a few letters of context and watch. With no memory, each language's letters look like their own alien code. Give it five characters of memory, and the languages converge.

Physicists would recognise the shape. Up close, every gas looks like chaos; from far enough away, they obey the same laws. Languages differ in their local details and agree on the large-scale budget.

Idea 3

The seesaw: order or endings

A language has to say who did what to whom somewhere. Finnish and Latin put it in word endings, so you can shuffle their words and lose little. Thai and Japanese put it in word order, so shuffling destroys a lot. When one side goes up, the other comes down, and the total stays put. This trade-off was published in 2017 across about 1,000 languages, and our data reproduced it.

Idea 4

What actually crosses between languages

A translation model read each verse three ways: alone, next to a random verse, and next to the real verse in English. The bits that the real English verse saves are the message the two texts share. What's left is packaging: everything the target language adds that English doesn't predict.

The message is about the same size every time: around 118 bits per verse, and about 130 between two English versions. Languages differ in their packaging, not in how much of the message comes across. Related languages don't share more, either. Finnish and Hungarian are cousins, and they barely beat Finnish and Arabic.

A message behaves like a protein. Many different codon spellings make the same protein, and many different languages carry the same message.

Idea 5

Poetry is where the packaging is the message

Lists of names and plain prose cross between languages most easily. Psalms cross least. In a poem, rhythm and sound are part of what's being said, so they can't be carried over.

The same chapters are hard in every language. They're also the ones where our meaning-maps disagree most. Three unrelated measurements point at the same passages, so the difficulty is in the thought, not in any particular language.

Idea 6

Every language has an accent

Everything so far came from translations. So we gathered 62 novels, French, German and English originals next to their translations, and asked whether a language leaves its own mark. It does, and the mark is identical whether a book was written in that language or translated into it. A translated novel takes on its new language's accent as fully as a native one.

How big the accent looks depends on how you listen. Counting which words keep company, it's about a third the size of the story's own mark. Neural models, which read whole sentences and so hear grammar too, find it two to three times the size of the story's. Authors leave a mark across their different books, and a faint one survives translation. The culture a book came from leaves at most a trace.

The accent lives in idiom: the habitual phrasings each language reaches for.

the first time
la première fois
English ties "time" to first and second. French uses a different word, so its "temps" never meets them.
aloud
à haute voix
French pairs "high" with "voice". English almost never does.
ask a question
poser une question
In English, asking and questions travel together. In French a question is "put".
Putting it together

The layers of a message

Stack everything, and a picture with layers comes out. Packaging changes a great deal and carries little. The message changes very little and carries most of what matters.

What next

Things we could build with this

A message distiller

Line up many renderings of one passage and keep only what all of them agree on. About 360 KB would hold the shared message of the whole Bible, a tenth of any single translation.

A message meter

Score any translation, summary or AI answer by how much of the source's message came across and where it was lost.

An "is this a language?" test

Check an unknown signal (whale codas, an undeciphered script) for a steady information rate and the order-or-endings seesaw.

A map of where meaning resists

Predict which passages will lose the most in translation, for translators, learners and poets.

How we measured, and what to keep in mind

Bits come from compressing written text (a character model that looks back six letters, checked with stronger compressors). The shared message comes from a 600M-parameter translation model scoring 2,346 held-out verses in 53 languages. Maps of meaning compare how 123 aligned concepts (man, day, hand, say, believe...) relate to each other inside each text. Keep in mind: this is writing, not speech, so spelling systems move the numbers; most cross-language texts are translations of one book; and the analyses were exploratory, not preregistered. The novels were checked with three neural models against an untrained control, which showed nothing.
